# The evidence behind each rule

## Sounds like talk

Mayer's personalization principle: learners did better on transfer tests when the words were
conversational rather than formal, in eleven of eleven tests with a median effect size of **d =
1.11** (Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*, 2009, ch. 13). The later count is fourteen of seventeen,
median d = 0.79 (Mayer, *The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*, 2014, ch. 14). The proposed
mechanism is social — the reader treats the writer as a conversational partner and engages in active
sense-making instead of passive reception. First and second person are part of the effect, not
decoration.

The voice evidence: Beck, McKeown & Worthy (*Reading Research Quarterly* 30(2), 1995) revised a
fourth-grade textbook passage for coherence and gave it a voice. That version beat the original and
the other revisions on recall and comprehension right after reading, and the delayed differences
were not significant (ERIC abstract).

## Length — why a long explanation is offered in parts

Users read about **20%** of the words on an average web page, by Nielsen's estimate from visit times
("How Little Do Users Read?", 2008). In an earlier study, 79% of test users always scanned a new
page and 16% read word by word ("How Users Read on the Web", 1997). In eyetracking, 81%, 71% and 63%
of users looked at the first three paragraphs and **only 32% at the fourth** ("Website Reading: It
(Sometimes) Does Happen", 2013). All three are nngroup.com articles by Jakob Nielsen.

LLMs are biased the wrong way here: in RLHF, reward gains came mostly from longer responses, and a
reward on length alone reproduced most of them (Singhal, Goyal, Xu & Durrett, "A Long Way to Go:
Investigating Length Correlations in RLHF", COLM 2024).

## Sentence length and voice

Sentence length and word length predict reading time. Graesser, McNamara & Kulikowich call the
Flesch-Kincaid measures, which are built from them, robust predictors of reading time ("Coh-Metrix:
Providing multilevel analyses of text characteristics", *Educational Researcher* 40(5), 2011). Words
per sentence correlate .86 with counts of Yngve word depths, a measure of syntactic depth (Bormuth
1966). By 1981 there were over 200 readability formulas, and a vocabulary measure and average
sentence length were the best predictors of difficulty (both as reported by DuBay, *The Principles
of Readability*, 2004, ERIC ED490073).

**The counter-finding matters more than the rule.** Across 20 Dutch texts read by 794 adolescents,
causal and contrastive connectives raised comprehension of the passage around them while additive
ones lowered it (Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders, *Discourse Processes* 56(5-6), 2019). And of 36
studies that rewrote texts to readability-formula variables, 19 found an effect on comprehension, 6
were mixed and 11 found none (Klare 1976, as reported by DuBay 2004). So brevity comes from cutting
content, never from cutting logic. The measured support covers causal and contrastive connectives.
Keeping the "and" that closes a list, as SKILL.md also asks, is judgment.

Non-canonical structures cost more: passives are "frequently and systematically misinterpreted,
especially when they express implausible ideas" (Ferreira, *Cognitive Psychology* 47, 2003).

Most plain-language guidance is unvalidated. A systematic review of plain-language summaries
reports: "We did not find empirical evidence to support most of the criteria we identified in the
PLS writing guidelines." (Stoll, Kerwer, Lieb & Chasiotis, "Plain language summaries: A systematic
review of theory, guidelines and empirical research", *PLOS ONE* 17(6), 2022). Shorter sentences,
active voice and defining jargon were among the criteria it found untested. The rules kept here
rest on the other evidence in this file, not on that review.

## Jargon you can't see

The curse of expertise, measured: experts, intermediate users and novices predicted how long novices
would take on a complex task, and "those with more expertise were worse predictors of novice
performance times and were resistant to debiasing techniques intended to reduce underestimation"
(Hinds, "The curse of expertise", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 5(2):205-221, 1999).
That the same blindness hides an expert's own jargon from them is judgment.

Bjork's fluency illusion explains the mechanism — a familiar topic feels effortless to think about,
and the mind reads that ease as the topic being simple (Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, "Self-regulated
learning: beliefs, techniques, and illusions", *Annual Review of Psychology* 64, 2013). Across
21,486 cave-research papers, a larger share of jargon in the title and abstract went with fewer
citations (Martínez & Mammola, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 288, 2021).

## Concrete before abstract, and bounded analogies

Comparing two cases beats studying one: in negotiation training, one experiment "showed a marked
advantage for comparing two cases over studying the 2 cases separately" (Gentner, Loewenstein &
Thompson, "Learning and transfer: A general role for analogical encoding", *Journal of Educational
Psychology* 95(2), 2003). The comparison helps learners abstract the shared schema, which is what
transfers. That a label on the shared structure helps further is judgment. Fading from concrete to
abstract is concreteness fading, which Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone review ("Concreteness fading in
mathematics and science instruction: a systematic review", *Educational Psychology Review* 26,
2014). That it works by highlighting the relational structure is judgment, since only the review's
record was checked here.

The failure mode is real: an analogy spreads a misconception when the learner generalises the wrong
similarity (Spiro, Feltovich, Coulson & Anderson, "Multiple analogies for complex concepts:
antidotes for analogy-induced misconception in advanced knowledge acquisition", in Vosniadou &
Ortony (eds.), *Similarity and Analogical Reasoning*, Cambridge University Press, 1989). So the
limit gets stated, not left implicit.

## Analogies — land the gist, then hand off

Two findings pull in opposite directions, and the handoff is what reconciles them.

**For carrying one coherent world:** Gentner's **systematicity principle** (Gentner,
"Structure-mapping: A theoretical framework for analogy", *Cognitive Science* 7(2), 1983) — people
prefer analogical matches whose relations are interconnected by higher-order relations, over an
equal number of matches that stand alone. Extended metaphors evoke a whole system of knowledge, the
way the computer metaphor carried encoding, storage and retrieval into how we describe memory.

**Against it:** Spiro's work on multiple analogies (Spiro et al. 1989, above) found that a single
striking analogy exerts a **reductive force** — the incomplete representation becomes the only
representation the learner holds, and understanding stops at the edge of the mapping. Students
taught with integrated *sets* of analogies developed more accurate understanding than those anchored
to one. His conclusion: no single analogy captures a complex system.

**The resolution here is the handoff, not the carried world.** Concreteness fading — concrete case
first, then the general version — fits both findings, and that fit is judgment. The analogy
carries first contact and then gives way to the real names, so the reader ends up holding the
mechanism rather than the metaphor. That handoff answers the reductive force without needing a
second analogy.

**One thing in the skill is derived rather than measured.** That the analogy's limit *is* the
handoff point is an operationalisation, not a finding, so it is judgment.

## Marking the edges of what you know

Judgment, not measurement. It comes from the same place as the jargon finding: fluency reads as
competence, and a reader cannot tell a traced claim from a plausible one unless the writer says
which it is. The curse-of-expertise finding suggests the writer often cannot tell either. So the
marking is stated per claim rather than assessed at the end.

## What is deliberately absent

**Format preference.** Matching instruction to a reader's preferred style — the meshing hypothesis —
lacks an adequate evidence base: few studies had the design needed to test it, and several of those
that did found results that flatly contradict it (Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork, "Learning
styles: Concepts and evidence", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9(3), 2008). Offering
bullets-versus-analogies as a comprehension setting would be that myth, so the format is fixed: the
Concise output style's.

**Reader-level calibration.** Cut deliberately: the skill asks at most one question, whether a long
answer comes whole or a part at a time, and never asks the reader's level. A novice benefits from
more signal, and an expert can learn more from text that makes them work. Techniques that help
inexperienced learners "can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used
with more experienced learners" (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller, "The expertise reversal
effect", *Educational Psychologist* 38(1), 2003). Readers who knew little about a domain gained
from a coherent text, while high-knowledge readers gained from a minimally coherent one (McNamara,
Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch, "Are good texts always better?", *Cognition and Instruction* 14(1),
1996). Worth revisiting if the skill ever asks the reader's level.
