# The evidence behind the quiz rules

Each section names a rule in [verbal.md](verbal.md), [quiz-card.md](quiz-card.md) or
[ask-user-question.md](ask-user-question.md) and the paper cited on it. The rules without a citation
rest on the operator's judgment, as their *(kstack: reason)* tags say.

## Ask for a guess

Rule: verbal.md, question step 2. When the person is unsure, ask for a guess.

Huelser & Metcalfe (2012) had people either study a cue and its target, or first guess the target
and then see it. When cue and target were related, the wrong guess followed by the correct answer
led to the best retention. When they were unrelated, the guess gave no benefit. People did not know
the guess had helped them, even right after the task.

Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012) found the same pattern for a retrieval attempt made before study. In
experiment 1, attempting to recall the target before studying it improved later cued recall for
related pairs, d = 0.94, and made no difference for unrelated pairs. A brief delay between the
attempt and the study trial removed the benefit (experiment 3).

In both studies the guess helped only when the cue pointed toward the answer. The step from those
cue and target pairs to a question about a tip is judgment.

## Give the right answer after a miss

Rules: verbal.md, question step 3. quiz-card.md, the explanation. ask-user-question.md, the
results message.

Rowland (2014) is a meta-analysis of testing against restudy. Testing helped more when the initial
test gave feedback, g = 0.73 (52 effect sizes), than when it did not, g = 0.39 (107 effect sizes).
The analysis coded whether feedback was given, not what it said. The one sentence that says why,
which each rule adds, is judgment.
